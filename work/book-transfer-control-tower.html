Book Transfer Control Tower

Part-way through a book transfer, is the deadline at risk, and what would change the answer? A simulation of one invented 420-household book that you can re-staff, stress and re-order.

The problem

When an advisor’s book moves between institutions, hundreds of households have to land by a fixed date and some transfers reject. A team lead needs to know mid-way whether the deadline is at risk.

My contribution

The question comes from my onboarding work, where I rank households by assets, age and rejection status. The model and its code were built with Claude, an AI model, at my direction.

The result

On this invented book the chance of finishing on time is about 100% with seven analysts and about 0% with five, and a rise in rejections from 15% to 25% cuts it to roughly 40% at seven. These are the model’s numbers, not a real firm’s.

Synthetic data Every household, dollar and rejection below is invented. No client or employer information is used.

Context

A transfer is finished when the assets have landed, not when the paperwork has been sent. Rejected transfers come back days later for a reason nobody planned for, so the remaining work is uncertain even when the remaining count is known.

This is the planning question behind the daily case lists I build at work, set up as a model so the reasoning can be inspected.

How to read it

  • Completion is measured in assets: the book is finished when 99% of its dollars have landed. Households done is shown beside it because the two can diverge.
  • Capacity is analysts × 2 households a day. A processed household either lands or is rejected; a rejected one returns to the queue 3 to 7 days later and can be rejected again.
  • The forecast runs the remaining days 240 times. The shaded band is the middle 80% of runs, and “chance of finishing on time” is the share of runs that finish by day 45.
  • Largest-first lands assets sooner because a few households hold most of the dollars. It does not finish more households, and the smallest ones wait longest.

A design choice

Simulate, or divide? The quick estimate is open households divided by daily capacity. It gives one finishing day and no sense of risk. The model simulates instead, because rejections re-enter the queue at random and it is the spread of outcomes that tells a team lead whether to ask for help.

The cost is that a simulation needs assumptions a division does not, and they are listed beside the controls so they can be challenged.

How it was built

AI did the building. This model was produced by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working at my direction. I asked for a project grounded in my onboarding work; Claude proposed the design, wrote the JavaScript and generated the invented book.

My role was to set the brief, review the result against how the work actually runs, and ask for the changes that made it explainable: visible assumptions, a baseline to compare against, and limits stated on the page.

Validation

  • The random number generator is seeded, so the same settings and seed always reproduce the same result. The seed is shown under the chart.
  • The model was tested at its limits: fewest and most analysts, lowest and highest rejection rate, both queue orders, and blank, non-numeric and out-of-range entries.
  • I am working through the code so that I can explain each assumption without notes. Until I have, treat the specific percentages as the model’s output, not my estimate.

Limitations

  • The book is invented. Household sizes, start dates and the mix of rejection reasons are inputs chosen for the model, not measurements.
  • Capacity is a fixed number per analyst. Real cases differ in effort, and people are not interchangeable.
  • Rejections are independent and equally likely for every household. In practice some account types reject far more often.
  • It models one book in isolation, with no competing work and no service-level commitments to smaller clients.
  • Any recommendation holds only inside these assumptions.
