Did T+1 settlement increase settlement fails?

In May 2024 North American markets halved the time allowed to settle a trade. Operations teams expected more failed settlements. This study tests whether that happened, using every fail reported by the U.S. clearing house over nearly five years.

The question

Did cutting the settlement cycle from two days to one increase failed settlements?

The method

A segmented regression on daily fails, twelve months either side of the change, controlling for trading volume, volatility and calendar effects. The same model is run on 117 placebo dates.

The finding

Two different measures, two answers. The stock of fails (their value, their number, and the share five or more days old) shows no detectable change. Short persistence does change: a new fail became about 3.4 points more likely to still be open on day two.

What it means for an operations team

The raw numbers mislead. Average fails were 23% higher in the year after T+1. Trading volume was higher too, and fails were already rising. Once those are accounted for, the change is +5% with a confidence interval that spans zero.

Ageing and second-day persistence are different measures. “Ageing” here means fails still open after five settlement days, which is where control problems start; it did not change. “Second-day persistence” means a new fail surviving one extra day; it rose. Fails that used to be repaired inside the settlement window now cross it by a day and are then cleared, so prevention has to happen on trade date.

Measure ageing, not counts. The daily count of fails moves with volume and the calendar, and more than doubles after quarterly index rebalancing. The share of fails ageing past a threshold is stable and a better control metric.

The limits are stated. Clearing-house data are net positions. A firm can have more internal breaks without its net position changing, so this cannot speak to client-level fails.
