Valuing Canadian National Railway

A discounted cash flow and peer valuation of Canadian National Railway, built from eight years of filed financial statements, with a formula-driven Excel workbook and a six-page memo.

The problem

What is CN worth on its own cash flows, how does that compare with what peers trade at, and what does today’s share price require you to believe?

My contribution

I chose the question and set the standard: public filings only, every number traceable, and no rating. The model, code and memo were built with Claude, an AI model, at my direction.

The result

The base case values CN at about C$112 a share against a price of C$179.80 on 2026-11-10. Peer multiples imply about C$210. The memo explains the gap. This is an exercise, not investment advice.

Context

CN is one of the largest North American freight railways and trades at a discount to the other listed Class I railroads. Railways earn high margins and also have to reinvest heavily in track and equipment, so reported earnings overstate the cash an owner can take out. That tension is what the model is built to show.

Sources

  • Financial statements: CN’s annual statements for FY2018–FY2025, US GAAP, in Canadian dollars, taken from the XBRL data filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (EDGAR).
  • Peers: FY2025 filings of Canadian Pacific Kansas City, Union Pacific, CSX and Norfolk Southern, also from EDGAR.
  • Risk-free rate: Government of Canada 10-year benchmark yield, Bank of Canada.
  • Share prices: Yahoo Finance closing prices on the valuation date.

A decision

Which capital expenditure to use. The value of a railway turns on how much it must reinvest. One option is to assume spending falls toward depreciation in the long run, which raises the valuation sharply. The model uses CN’s actual five-year average spending instead, because CN does not disclose a split between maintenance and growth spending and I did not want the answer to rest on a number I could not source.

The memo says plainly that this is the swing factor, and the sensitivity tables show how far the value moves.

No rating. The work ends in a statement of what the market price assumes: about 4.4% growth forever, or a 6.0% cost of capital. It does not end in a buy or sell call or a price target.

How it was built

AI did the building. The data pipeline, the valuation model, the Excel workbook and the memo was produced by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working at my direction. I asked for a company valuation on real public data with a model, a memo and reproducible code; Claude chose the company, wrote the Python and drafted the memo.

My role was to set the brief and the constraints, read the output and decide what is published. I am working through the workbook so I can explain each assumption; my own hand-built valuation of a second company is in progress.

Validation

  • A separate script recalculates the Excel workbook with an independent formula engine and compares 18 outputs with the Python model. All 18 agree.
  • The forecast balance sheet balances in every year, checked by a cell in the workbook.
  • The memo is generated from the results file, so its numbers cannot drift from the model.
  • Three years of statements were missing from the SEC’s summary data feed, so the script reads those filings directly, and the code checks that FY2025 has no gaps.

Limitations

  • The valuation date is fixed. Prices and yields have moved since, and re-running the code changes every figure.
  • Deferred taxes are treated as cash taxes and debt is taken at book value.
  • Peer multiples use trailing FY2025 results and are not adjusted for pensions or leases. One peer, Norfolk Southern, is subject to an agreed merger, which affects its price.
  • The forecast uses nine top-down drivers, not a build-up from volumes and prices by commodity.
  • An equity risk premium of 5% is an assumption. A lower one would close part of the gap to the share price.
