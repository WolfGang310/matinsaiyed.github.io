Canadian National Railway (TSX: CNR)

What is the franchise worth, and what is the share price assuming?

Summary. CN is one of the highest-quality freight franchises in North America, and its shares trade at a discount to other Class I railroads. A discounted cash flow model built from eight years of filed financials nevertheless values the equity at about C$112 per share in the base case, 38% below the C$179.80 market price, and at C$152 even in the upside case. Peer multiples point the other way, implying roughly C$210.

The two methods disagree because the whole sector is priced richly. To justify today’s price the model needs either perpetual growth of 4.4% a year, a cost of capital of 6.0%, or five years of 13.1% annual revenue growth. My conclusion is that CN is cheap relative to peers but expensive relative to its own cash flows, and that the gap comes down to how much of its earnings must be reinvested in the network.

1. The business

CN operates a rail network connecting Canada’s Atlantic and Pacific coasts with the U.S. Gulf Coast. Freight railroads are natural duopolies or monopolies on most lanes: track cannot be replicated economically, and for bulk commodities over long distances there is no competitive substitute. That shows up as pricing power and high margins. It also shows up as heavy, non-discretionary capital spending, because the network has to be maintained whether volumes grow or not.

From FY2018 to FY2025 revenue grew from C$14,321m to C$17,304m, a compound rate of 2.7%. The operating ratio, operating expenses as a share of revenue and the industry’s standard efficiency measure, was 61.9% in FY2025 (Figure 1). Diluted earnings per share rose from C$5.87 to C$7.57, faster than net income, because the diluted share count fell from 738m to 624m.

Figure 2 is the most important chart in this memo. Over eight years CN generated C$26.1bn of free cash flow after capital expenditure and returned C$34.3bn to shareholders through dividends and buybacks. The difference was funded with debt, which rose from C$12,569m to C$21,206m. Capital expenditure has averaged 19.4% of revenue over the last five years against depreciation of 11.2%. Reported earnings therefore overstate the cash an owner can take out.

3. Cost of capital

The cost of equity is 8.50%, from a risk-free rate of 3.77% (Government of Canada 10-year benchmark), an equity risk premium of 5.0%, and a beta of 0.95. The beta is a 60-month regression against the S&P/TSX Composite (0.92), adjusted toward one. The pre-tax cost of debt is 4.34%, FY2025 interest expense over average debt. At market-value weights the weighted average cost of capital is 7.66%.

4. Discounted cash flow

Free cash flows for FY2026–FY2030 are discounted with a mid-year convention, and a Gordon-growth terminal value assumes 2.5% perpetual growth. Enterprise value is C$83,058m. Subtracting C$21,206m of debt, adding C$350m of cash and C$3,706m for the after-tax pension surplus gives equity of C$65,908m, or C$107 per share at the balance sheet date and C$112 rolled forward to the price date. Terminal value is 79% of enterprise value, which is typical for a long-lived infrastructure asset and is the reason the sensitivity tables matter more than the point estimate.

5. Comparable companies

The four other publicly traded Class I railroads trade at a median of 17.6 times FY2025 EBITDA and 27.6 times earnings. CN trades at 15.4 and 23.8 times, a discount of 12% on EV/EBITDA despite an operating ratio better than three of the four. Applying peer medians to CN’s FY2025 results implies C$210–213 per share.

6. Reconciling the two answers

A relative valuation says CN is worth more than its price, and an intrinsic valuation says it is worth less. Both are correct on their own terms. Multiples only say that CN is cheaper than its peers, not that any of them are cheap. The reverse DCF makes the market’s assumptions explicit. Holding everything else at the base case, the share price is justified by any one of:

  • terminal growth of 4.4% a year forever, above long-run nominal GDP growth;
  • a cost of capital of 6.0%, about 1.7 points below my estimate;
  • revenue growth of 13.1% a year for five years, against 2.7% achieved since 2018.

None of these is impossible, and a combination of smaller moves in each would do it. Investors may reasonably view a railroad as a long-duration, inflation-protected asset deserving a lower discount rate than CAPM produces. But the model shows what is being paid for, and I would want a specific reason to believe it before paying.

7. Risks and limitations

  • Capital intensity is the swing factor. If maintenance capex is closer to depreciation than to recent spending, base value rises materially. I use reported spending because CN does not disclose a maintenance/growth split.
  • Macro and trade. Volumes depend on commodity cycles, cross-border trade policy and labour disruptions, none of which is modelled explicitly.
  • Simplifications. Deferred taxes are treated as cash taxes, which understates cash flow slightly. Debt is taken at book value. Peer multiples are not adjusted for pensions, leases or one-off items. The comparison uses trailing FY2025 results, not forward estimates.
  • Timing. The balance sheet is at 31 December 2025 and the price is 10 months later; value is rolled forward at the cost of equity less dividends.
